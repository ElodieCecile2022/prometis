Scams

The most common scams, and how to beat them

26 scam techniques sorted by channel. For each one: how the trap works, and the reflex that defuses it.

By phone

The fake bank adviser

The trap: A call shows your bank's number and reports suspicious payments. You are asked to approve an operation or read out a text-message code.

The reflex: I hang up and call the number on the back of my card.

Find out more about: The fake bank adviser

Find out more about: The fake bank adviser

Example: “Hello, this is your bank's fraud department. A payment of £1,250 is being made from your account. To block it, approve the operation that is about to appear in your app.”

Warning signs:

  • you are asked for a code received by text, or to “approve” an operation in your app in order to cancel it;
  • you are offered to transfer your money to a “safe account”;
  • you are rushed and asked not to hang up.

If you have already fallen for it: Block your card, call your bank on its usual number, dispute the operations in writing and report it to the police.

Fake customer service

The trap: A fake agent from a phone operator, online shop or energy supplier tells you about a refund or a problem with your account.

The reflex: I contact customer service myself through the official website or app.

Find out more about: Fake customer service

Find out more about: Fake customer service

Example: You search online for a company's customer service and call the first number you find. The fake agent says: “To refund you, I'm going to take control of your computer.”

Warning signs:

  • the number comes from an ad, a social network or a text message, not from the official website;
  • you are asked to install a remote-access app;
  • you are asked for your bank details to “refund” you.

If you have already fallen for it: Uninstall the remote-access software, block your card if you gave it, and change your passwords from another device.

Fake tech support

The trap: A window freezes your screen: “Computer infected, call this number”. The fake technician takes remote control and gets paid.

The reflex: I don't call. I restart the computer. No software company contacts you like this.

Find out more about: Fake tech support

Find out more about: Fake tech support

Example: A window opens with an alarm sound: “Your computer is infected. Call Microsoft support immediately on 01…”

Warning signs:

  • a message that freezes the screen and shows a number to call;
  • a “technician” who demands payment, sometimes in gift cards;
  • a request for remote access to your computer.

If you have already fallen for it: Switch off the computer, block your card if you paid, change your passwords and report it: reportfraud.police.uk (UK) or ReportFraud.ftc.gov (US).

The fake police officer

The trap: A so-called investigator explains that your account is being used for fraud and asks you to put your money “somewhere safe”.

The reflex: The police never ask for a transfer. I hang up and call 999 (UK) or 911 (US) in an emergency.

Find out more about: The fake police officer

Find out more about: The fake police officer

Example: “This is the financial crimes unit. Your bank adviser is suspected of fraud. To protect your money, we will give you a holding account. Don't tell your branch.”

Warning signs:

  • you are asked to keep it secret, even from your bank;
  • you are asked to withdraw or transfer money, or hand over jewellery;
  • you are called back several times to keep up the pressure.

If you have already fallen for it: Stop all contact, call your bank on its usual number, then report it: Report Fraud on 0300 123 2040 (UK) or ReportFraud.ftc.gov (US).

Fake relatives and cloned voices

The trap: A loved one in distress calls or writes from a new number. Their voice may be imitated by artificial intelligence.

The reflex: I call back on their usual number. Family password.

Find out more about: Fake relatives and cloned voices

Find out more about: Fake relatives and cloned voices

Example: A voice message from your grandson, in tears: “Gran, I've had an accident, my phone is broken, I need £800 right now. Don't tell Dad.”

Warning signs:

  • a new number, an emergency and a request for secrecy;
  • an unusual payment: transfer to a third party, vouchers, prepaid cards;
  • the voice sounds right, but the story doesn't quite add up.

If you have already fallen for it: Call your loved one on their usual number. Agree on a family password for these situations now.

Premium-rate missed calls

The trap: A brief call from an unknown, often foreign, number tempts you to call back a very expensive number.

The reflex: I don't call back a number I don't know.

Find out more about: Premium-rate missed calls

Find out more about: Premium-rate missed calls

Example: A one-second call from a foreign number or a premium-rate number (09 in the UK, 900 in the US). If you call back, every minute is charged at a very high rate.

Warning signs:

  • a very short call, at an unusual time;
  • a foreign country code you don't recognise;
  • a voice message urging you to call back urgently.

If you have already fallen for it: Check your next bill, contact your operator and report the number by texting “Call” followed by the number to 7726 (UK and US).

By text and messaging apps

Fake delivery texts

The trap: “Your parcel is on hold, pay £1.99”. The link opens a fake site that steals your card.

The reflex: I don't click. I forward the text to 7726 (UK and US).

Find out more about: Fake delivery texts

Find out more about: Fake delivery texts

Example: “Your parcel could not be delivered. Pay the £1.99 redelivery fee here: parcel-track-uk.com”

Warning signs:

  • you aren't expecting a parcel, or the carrier doesn't match;
  • the link doesn't lead to the carrier's official website;
  • you are asked for a small sum: it's bait to get your card number.

If you have already fallen for it: If you entered your card details, block your card immediately and keep an eye on your accounts.

Fake fines and fake refunds

The trap: Unpaid fine or road toll, tax refund (HMRC, IRS), benefits or health card to renew: same trap, different setting.

The reflex: I always go through the official website, never the link.

Find out more about: Fake fines and fake refunds

Find out more about: Fake fines and fake refunds

Example: “HMRC: a tax refund of £147 is waiting for you. Confirm your details within 48 hours.” Or: “Unpaid toll notice, penalty fee tomorrow.”

Warning signs:

  • the link doesn't end in .gov.uk or .gov, or the organisation's official address;
  • you are asked for your bank details to receive money;
  • a very short deadline.

If you have already fallen for it: Don't pay anything through the link. Check on the official website (gov.uk in the UK, usa.gov or irs.gov in the US). If you gave your card details, block your card.

“Mum, I've changed my number”

The trap: On WhatsApp or by text, a “child” asks you to pay an urgent bill.

The reflex: I call my child on their old number.

Find out more about: “Mum, I've changed my number”

Find out more about: “Mum, I've changed my number”

Example: “Hi Mum, it's me, I've changed my number. Can you do me a favour? I've got a bill to pay today and my banking app isn't working.”

Warning signs:

  • the message comes from an unknown number;
  • you are asked to pay on your child's behalf, often to an IBAN that isn't theirs;
  • they avoid calls: “I can't talk right now”.

If you have already fallen for it: Call your child on their old number. If you made a transfer, contact your bank immediately.

Fake job offers

The trap: Simple “paid tasks” (liking, rating), then you are asked to pay money upfront to unlock your earnings.

The reflex: A real employer never makes you pay.

Find out more about: Fake job offers

Find out more about: Fake job offers

Example: “Earn £50 to £200 a day from home: all you have to do is like videos.” The first earnings are paid, then you are asked to “top up your account” to unlock a large sum.

Warning signs:

  • unsolicited contact on WhatsApp, Telegram or by text;
  • work that's too simple to be paid so well;
  • you are asked to pay money upfront or pay in cryptocurrency.

If you have already fallen for it: Stop paying, keep the messages and report it to the police. Never send your identity documents.

By email and post

Phishing

The trap: An email in the colours of a bank, the tax office or a well-known website asks you to “verify your account”.

The reflex: I look at the sender's address. I don't click.

Find out more about: Phishing

Find out more about: Phishing

Example: “Your account will be suspended in 24 hours. Click here to confirm your details.” The email uses your bank's or the tax office's logo.

Warning signs:

  • the sender's address doesn't match the real website;
  • a link to a strange address (hover over it without clicking);
  • a threat, a very short deadline or spelling mistakes.

If you have already fallen for it: Change the password concerned straight away, turn on two-factor authentication, and block your card if you gave it.

The fake bank letter

The trap: A convincing paper letter asks you to scan a QR code or call a number to “secure” your account.

The reflex: I call my branch on the number I know.

Find out more about: The fake bank letter

Find out more about: The fake bank letter

Example: A letter on your bank's letterhead: “Following a security update, scan this QR code to reactivate your card.”

Warning signs:

  • a QR code or phone number that doesn't appear on your usual documents;
  • a request to “reactivate”, “secure” or “verify” your account;
  • an unexpected letter, even if it looks very official.

If you have already fallen for it: Call your branch on the number you know. If you entered your login details, change them and tell your bank.

Booby-trapped QR codes

The trap: A fake QR code stuck on a parking meter, a menu or a letter leads to a fraudulent payment site.

The reflex: I check the address shown before paying.

Find out more about: Booby-trapped QR codes

Find out more about: Booby-trapped QR codes

Example: On a parking meter, a QR code sticker has been stuck over the original. It leads to a fake payment site.

Warning signs:

  • a QR code that is stuck on, damaged or placed over another one;
  • the address shown after scanning doesn't match the official service;
  • you are asked for more information than necessary.

If you have already fallen for it: If you paid, block your card and report the sticker to the car park operator or the town hall.

CEO fraud

The trap: A fake executive urgently and discreetly requests an exceptional transfer.

The reflex: I check through another channel, even if it's “confidential”.

Find out more about: CEO fraud

Find out more about: CEO fraud

Example: Email from the “CEO”: “We are finalising a confidential acquisition. I need an urgent transfer of £48,000. Don't go through the usual procedure.”

Warning signs:

  • the request bypasses internal procedures;
  • urgency, flattery and confidentiality;
  • an email address very close to the real one, but different.

If you have already fallen for it: Tell your bank immediately to try to block the transfer, then your management, and report it to the police. In business, require two approvals for any unusual transfer.

Fake bank details and fake invoices

The trap: A supplier “changes bank” and sends new bank details. Payments go to the scammer.

The reflex: I confirm any change of bank details by phone.

Find out more about: Fake bank details and fake invoices

Find out more about: Fake bank details and fake invoices

Example: “Hello, following a change of bank, please use our new details to pay our invoices from now on.” The message seems to come from your usual supplier.

Warning signs:

  • a change of IBAN announced only by email;
  • an account located in another country;
  • pressure to pay quickly.

If you have already fallen for it: Call your bank to try to recall the transfer, alert your supplier on the number you know, and report it to the police.

Sextortion emails

The trap: A message claims to have hacked your webcam and demands a ransom in cryptocurrency.

The reflex: I don't pay, I don't reply. I report it.

Find out more about: Sextortion emails

Find out more about: Sextortion emails

Example: “I hacked your computer and filmed your screen. Pay £900 in bitcoin within 48 hours or I'll send the video to your contacts. Your password is: …”

Warning signs:

  • a generic blackmail message, often sent to thousands of people;
  • an old password quoted to scare you: it comes from a data leak;
  • payment in cryptocurrency.

If you have already fallen for it: Don't pay and don't reply. Change the password quoted, forward the email to report@phishing.gov.uk (UK) or report it on ic3.gov (US).

Accounts and identity

SIM swapping

The trap: The scammer pretends to be you with your mobile operator and gets a new SIM card. They receive your text-message codes.

The reflex: My phone has no signal for no reason? I call my operator and my bank.

Find out more about: SIM swapping

Find out more about: SIM swapping

Example: Your phone suddenly shows “No service”. Meanwhile, the scammer, who has obtained a new SIM card in your name, receives your bank codes by text.

Warning signs:

  • a sudden, lasting loss of signal;
  • a text or email from your operator announcing a SIM card change you didn't request;
  • unexpected password reset emails.

If you have already fallen for it: Call your operator from another phone, then your bank. Change your passwords and report it to the police.

Account hacking

The trap: You are asked to pass on a code received by text “by mistake”: it's the code for your WhatsApp or your email.

The reflex: A code you receive is never shared. I turn on two-factor authentication.

Find out more about: Account hacking

Find out more about: Account hacking

Example: A message from one of your contacts: “Sorry, I sent you a code by text by mistake, can you send it back?” Their account has been hacked, and the code lets them take over yours.

Warning signs:

  • someone asks you for a code you received;
  • an unusual message from a loved one;
  • a login alert from an unknown device.

If you have already fallen for it: Log back in to regain control of your account, turn on two-step verification and warn your contacts.

Identity theft

The trap: With your stolen documents or data, the scammer takes out loans or opens accounts in your name.

The reflex: I never send identity documents without a watermark. I report it quickly.

Find out more about: Identity theft

Find out more about: Identity theft

Example: To “confirm” a rental or a job offer, you are asked for a copy of your ID card, your tax notice and your bank details. A few weeks later, you receive letters about a loan you never took out.

Warning signs:

  • you are asked for too many documents, too early;
  • letters or direct debits you don't recognise;
  • an unexplained loan refusal.

If you have already fallen for it: Report it to the police, keep the receipt and alert the organisations concerned. In the US, go to IdentityTheft.gov for a recovery plan. In future, add a watermark (“copy for…”, date) to any document you send.

All the steps are detailed in the sheet “What to do if I'm a victim?”: UK version (PDF) · US version (PDF).

Data leaks

The trap: Your emails and passwords circulate after a website is hacked. They are used to prepare targeted scams.

The reflex: A different password for every site. A password manager helps.

Find out more about: Data leaks

Find out more about: Data leaks

Example: A website where you have an account announces it has been hacked. A few days later, you receive emails or calls from people who seem to know you very well.

Warning signs:

  • a data leak notification from a website or company;
  • very personalised messages from strangers;
  • login attempts on your accounts.

If you have already fallen for it: Change the password for the site concerned and everywhere else you used it, turn on two-factor authentication and be extra careful with calls.

Online and on social media

Fake online shops

The trap: Slashed prices, a brand-new site, dubious reviews: you pay, nothing arrives.

The reflex: I check the site and pay by card, never by bank transfer.

Find out more about: Fake online shops

Find out more about: Fake online shops

Example: A site offers branded trainers at −70%. The site is new, has no legal notice, and payment is by bank transfer.

Warning signs:

  • abnormally low prices;
  • no legal notice, no address, reviews that can't be found or are all identical;
  • payment only by bank transfer or prepaid card.

If you have already fallen for it: If you paid by card, contact your bank to dispute the operation. Report it: reportfraud.police.uk (UK) or ReportFraud.ftc.gov (US).

Fake buyer scams

The trap: On a classified ads site, a “buyer” sends a “secure payment” link that steals your card.

The reflex: I stay on the site's messaging. I never enter my card details to receive money.

Find out more about: Fake buyer scams

Find out more about: Fake buyer scams

Example: You're selling a piece of furniture online. A “buyer” writes: “I'm paying through the secure payment service, click this link to receive the money.” The link asks for your card number.

Warning signs:

  • you are asked for your card details to receive money;
  • the buyer wants to leave the site's messaging for WhatsApp or email;
  • the buyer asks no questions about the item.

If you have already fallen for it: Block your card if you entered it, report the profile to the ads site and report it to the police.

Fake investments

The trap: Crypto, gold, an “exceptional” savings account, often promoted by a fake ad featuring a celebrity.

The reflex: I check the regulator: FCA Warning List (UK), Investor.gov (US). Too good to be true.

Find out more about: Fake investments

Find out more about: Fake investments

Example: An ad shows a well-known personality recommending a “guaranteed” investment at 10% a month. An “adviser” then calls you back very regularly.

Warning signs:

  • a high return presented as risk-free;
  • a fake ad featuring a celebrity, sometimes a faked video;
  • pressure to invest more, and withdrawals that are impossible.

If you have already fallen for it: Stop all payments, gather the evidence and report it to the police. Check the FCA Warning List (UK) or Investor.gov (US). Beware of offers to “recover” your funds.

Romance scams

The trap: An online relationship develops over weeks, then come the requests for money.

The reflex: I never send money to someone I have never met.

Find out more about: Romance scams

Find out more about: Romance scams

Example: After several weeks of tender messages, the person you met online has a problem: a plane ticket to pay for, customs fees, a hospital stay…

Warning signs:

  • the person always refuses video calls or meetings;
  • they live or work far away (abroad, on assignment…);
  • requests for money start small, then grow.

If you have already fallen for it: Stop the payments, talk to someone close to you, keep the messages and report it to the police. Victim Support (08 08 16 89 111, UK) or the AARP Fraud Watch Network Helpline (877-908-3360, US) can help you.

Fake rentals

The trap: An ideal home, a landlord “abroad”, a deposit to pay before any viewing.

The reflex: No viewing, no payment.

Find out more about: Fake rentals

Find out more about: Fake rentals

Example: A well-located flat with a low rent. The landlord lives abroad and asks you to pay the deposit before sending you the keys.

Warning signs:

  • rent well below the market rate;
  • no viewing possible, or a viewing that keeps being postponed;

If you have already fallen for it: Contact your bank to try to block the transfer, keep the ad and the messages, and report it to the police.

Ransomware

The trap: Malicious software encrypts the files of a business or an individual and demands a ransom.

The reflex: Regular backups, updates, and an offline copy of your files.

Find out more about: Ransomware

Find out more about: Ransomware

Example: One morning, all your files are unreadable and a message appears: “Your data is encrypted. Pay to get it back.”

Warning signs:

  • files that change name or extension;
  • a computer that is suddenly very slow;
  • a ransom message on the screen.

If you have already fallen for it: Disconnect the device from the network, don't pay the ransom, report it to Report Fraud (UK) or IC3 (US). Then restore your data from your backups.
