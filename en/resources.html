The videos

Phone

The fake bank adviser in 90 seconds

1 min 21 s
Read the transcript

Hello, I'm Camille. Today: the fake bank adviser scam, one of the costliest scams around.

Your phone rings. The screen shows your bank's number. On the line, a calm voice tells you there are suspicious payments on your account.

To “block” the fraud, you are asked to read out a code received by text, or to approve an operation in your banking app. But that code blocks nothing: it approves a payment. And that payment goes straight to the scammer.

There are other versions. You may be asked to transfer your money to a so-called safe account. Or to hand your card to a courier who comes to your home. Or to install an app that gives access to your phone.

Remember just one thing: your bank will never ask you to approve an operation in order to cancel it. If in doubt, hang up, and call back yourself on the number on the back of your card.

If you've already been caught out, act fast. Block your card straight away, using your banking app or the number on the back of your card. In the UK, you can also call 159 to reach your bank safely. Call your bank on its usual number. Then report the fraud: to Report Fraud in the UK, or to the FTC in the US.

Remember: two safeguards are better than one. That was Camille, for WhitePaw. See you soon!

Texts

Fake parcel texts: spotting the trap link

55 s
Read the transcript

Hello, I'm Camille. Today, I'll show you how to spot a fake delivery text.

Here is a typical message. First clue: it comes from an ordinary phone number. Second clue: you're asked for a small sum. It's bait to get your card number. Third clue: the link doesn't lead to the carrier's official website.

The right reflex? Never click the link. If you're expecting a parcel, check directly on the carrier's official website. And report the message by forwarding it, free of charge, to 7726, in the UK and the US.

Already clicked? If you entered your bank card details, block your card immediately. Then keep a close eye on your accounts.

Accounts

SIM swapping explained simply

1 min 12 s · subtitled
Read the transcript

Hello, I'm Camille. Today, I'll explain a little-known but formidable scam: SIM swapping.

Your SIM card is your phone number. And your number receives the security codes your bank sends you by text.

SIM swapping is simple: a scammer pretends to be you with your mobile operator. They get a new SIM card, in your name.

Your old card is deactivated: suddenly, your phone has no signal. Meanwhile, the scammer receives your calls and your codes. They can empty your account.

How do they know your details? Often through a fake text, a fake call, or a data leak.

Here are three reflexes to protect yourself. One: never give personal information to someone who contacts you. Two: ask your mobile operator what protections exist against SIM card changes. Three: when possible, use an authenticator app rather than codes sent by text.

And if your phone suddenly has no signal for no reason? Call your operator straight away from another phone, then your bank.

Remember: distrust is the mother of safety. That was Camille, for WhitePaw. See you soon!

Internet

Fake tech support: the scary pop-up

57 s
Read the transcript

Hello, I'm Camille. Today, let's talk about the fake alert that freezes your computer.

You're browsing the internet. Suddenly, a window fills the screen, with an alarm. It says your computer is infected, and shows a number to call. On the phone, a fake technician takes control of your computer, then makes you pay for a fake repair.

What you need to know. Major software companies never contact you through this kind of window. The message comes from a web page, not from your computer. Close the browser. If it stays frozen, switch the computer off, then on again.

Have you already called, or let someone take control of your computer? Uninstall the software you were asked to install. Block your card if you paid. And change your passwords from another device. Then report what happened.

Two safeguards are better than one. That was Camille, for WhitePaw. See you soon!

You get a call, or a voice message, from a loved one in distress, from an unknown number. The voice is perfectly imitated: a few seconds of recording found online are enough. They have an urgent problem, and they ask you for money, in secret.

Post

The fake bank letter and the QR code

54 s
Read the transcript

Hello, I'm Camille. Today: when the scam arrives by post.

You receive a letter with your bank's logo. It looks very official. You're asked to scan a QR code, to reactivate your card, or secure your account. A deadline pushes you to act fast. The QR code leads to a fake website that imitates your bank's, and steals your login details.

The same trap exists in the street. Fake QR codes are sometimes stuck on parking meters, to make you pay on a fake website.

The right reflexes. For a letter, call your branch on the number you already know. After scanning, always check the website address before typing anything. And be wary of QR codes stuck over another one, or damaged.

Business

Fake bank details fraud: the business procedure

1 min 17 s
Read the transcript

Hello, I'm Camille. Today, I'm talking to businesses: fake bank details fraud, and how to protect yourself.

An email arrives, apparently from one of your regular suppliers. It announces a change of bank, and gives new bank details. The address looks like the real one. And you're asked to be quick. The next transfers then go to the scammer's account. Often, the supplier's mailbox has been hacked.

A variant: CEO fraud. A fake executive urgently requests an exceptional, confidential transfer, outside the usual procedures.

Here is the procedure to put in place. One: every change of bank details is confirmed by phone. Two: call the supplier's number you already know, never the one given in the message. Three: unusual transfers are approved by two people. Four: an urgent, confidential request always triggers a check.

If a fraudulent transfer has already gone, every minute counts. Call your bank immediately to try to recall it. Alert the supplier and your management. Then report it to the police.

Two safeguards are better than one. That was Camille, for WhitePaw. See you soon!

Internet

Fake investments: the telltale signs

1 min 11 s
Read the transcript

Hello, I'm Camille. Today: fake investments, those deals that are too good to be true.

It often starts with an ad on social media, featuring a well-known personality. Sometimes, the video is faked with artificial intelligence. You leave your details. A very friendly adviser calls you back, and offers you an exceptional investment.

Here are the telltale signs. A high return, presented as guaranteed and risk-free. Pressure to invest quickly, and more and more. Gains shown on a website, but impossible to withdraw. And when you try to get your money back, new fees to pay.

Remember the Crow and the Fox: every flatterer lives at the expense of those who listen. A return that's too good to be true… is false.

Before investing, always check. Check the regulator: the FCA Warning List in the UK, or Investor.gov in the US. Ask your bank for advice. And if someone offers to recover your lost money for a fee, it's another scam.

React

I've been scammed: 4 things to do right away

1 min 53 s
Read the transcript

Hello, I'm Camille. If you're the victim of a scam, there's no need to feel ashamed: scammers are professionals. What matters is acting fast. Here are four steps.

Step one: block. Block your card straight away, using your banking app or the number on the back of your card. In the UK, you can also call 159 to reach your bank safely. If you made a transfer, call your bank immediately to try to stop it. Then change your passwords, starting with your email.

Step two: keep the evidence. Don't delete anything: messages, emails, phone numbers. Take screenshots. And write down the date, the time, and what you were told.

Step three: report it. In the UK, contact Report Fraud on 0300 123 2040, or at reportfraud.police.uk. In the US, report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and online crime to the FBI at IC3.gov.

Step four: ask for a refund. Write to your bank, without delay, to dispute the operations you didn't authorise. In the UK, you have up to thirteen months to claim. In the US, report it within sixty days of the statement that shows it. If your bank refuses, make a formal complaint, then contact the ombudsman or the regulator.

You can also get help. In the UK, Victim Support listens on 08 08 16 89 111, day and night. In the US, the AARP Fraud Watch Network helpline answers on 877-908-3360, for everyone. Finally, beware of anyone who promises to recover your money for a fee: that's another scam.

Remember: distrust is the mother of safety. That was Camille, for WhitePaw. See you soon!

Your answers and the right reflexes

  1. False. The number displayed can be faked. Hang up and call your bank's official number back.
  2. I hang up and call my bank myself. The text-message code is used to approve an operation, never to cancel one. Giving it means authorising the scammer's payment.
  3. No, never. No bank sends a courier to collect a card, even one that has been cut up.
  4. I don't click, and I forward the text to 7726. The link leads to a fake site that steals bank details. In the UK and the US, 7726 lets you report scam texts free of charge.
  5. I call my child on their old number. Calling back on the usual number, or asking a question only your loved one knows, defeats the impersonation.
  6. Urgency: “You must act right now”. Urgency, fear and secrecy are the scammers' levers: they stop you from thinking.
  7. Very probably a scam. No real investment guarantees such a return. Check the regulator: FCA Warning List (UK) or Investor.gov (US).
  8. No. This information can be found or bought after a data leak.
  9. I block my card and call my bank. Acting fast increases the chances of blocking or recovering the money. Shame wastes time.
  10. I hang up and call the official number back myself. I hang up and call back the number I know myself.
